# Unreleased

## Highlights

- **Sending to an actor from outside the actor system is twice as fast**, with one sender and with one sender per core, and spreading messages from one sender over many actors is half again as fast.
- **Spawning an actor is a quarter faster, and a tree of a million short-lived actors is built in half the time and a third less memory.**
- **One sender spreading messages over many idle actors is two and a half times faster**, because a send to an idle actor no longer wakes a worker per message.

## Performance

- **Sending to an actor from outside the actor system is twice as fast.** `Tell` and `Ask` from outside an actor took two read locks per message, one on the target's fields and one on the actor system, and the lock counters sat on a cache line the receiving actor reads for every message. Both reads are now lock-free, and the fields of a `PID` are grouped so that what senders read and what the receiving actor writes never share a cache line. In the benchmarks on an 8-core machine, one sender delivers 26 million messages/sec, from 13 million (`BenchmarkTell`), eight senders on one actor 11.8 million, from 6 million (`BenchmarkTellParallel`), one sender spreading messages over 60 actors 6.1 million, from 3.8 million (`BenchmarkForkJoinThroughput`), and eight concurrent asks on one actor 2.2 million, from 2.1 million (`BenchmarkAskParallel`). Messages between actors, which took neither lock, are unchanged.
- **One sender spreading messages over many idle actors is two and a half times faster.** A `Tell` from outside the actor system to an actor that no worker was running handed the actor to a parked worker for every message, which woke a worker per message; the cost of a wake-up grows with the number of processors, and this scenario got slower with every core added. The actor now goes on the dispatcher's ready queue and a worker is woken only when none is looking for work, so a worker that is awake drains a stream of such sends and a woken worker that finds a backlog wakes the next one, the rule the Go scheduler applies to its spinning threads. A caller that blocks for the answer, an `Ask` or a grain call, still hands its actor to a parked worker directly, and a child spawned from its parent's turn is queued on that turn's worker. In the benchmarks on an 8-core machine, one sender spreading messages over 60 actors delivers 14 million messages/sec, from 6.1 million (`BenchmarkForkJoinThroughput`), the Skynet tree is built in 2.3 s, from 3.0 s (`BenchmarkSkynet`), and a spawn followed by a stop is 6% faster (`BenchmarkSpawnStop`); every other scenario is unchanged.
- **Spawning an actor is a quarter faster, and a tree of a million short-lived actors is built in half the time and a third less memory.** A spawn followed by a stop takes 3.4 µs and 25 allocations, from 4.7 µs and 27 (`BenchmarkSpawnStop`); the Skynet tree of 1.1 million actors is built and torn down in 3.4 s and 3.1 GiB, from 6.3 s and 4.9 GiB (`BenchmarkSkynet`). The spawns of different names no longer serialise on one mutex, an actor's incarnation identity is drawn from the runtime's random generator instead of a system call per spawn and is still a version 4 UUID, an actor name is checked by a loop over its bytes instead of a regular expression, an actor's first mailbox segment holds 8 messages in 640 bytes instead of 32 in 2 KiB and grows to full segments only when a backlog needs them, the `ActorChildCreated` event is built only when the topic has a subscriber, a stopping actor builds its `Terminated` only when something receives it and tells the death watch only in a cluster, where that removes its registry record, and registering an actor for passivation wakes the passivation loop only when the actor's deadline is the earliest. Settings, errors and event delivery are unchanged.

## Documentation

- **One benchmark suite.** `benchmark` now holds eighteen scenarios: fire-and-forget and request/response sends from outside the system and between actors, the Savina ping-pong, thread ring, fork-join and big exchanges, Skynet, spawn and stop, the live heap per idle actor and grain, and the grain asks and activation. Every benchmark reports throughput, time, bytes and allocations per operation; `benchmark/cmd/table` renders result files as the six tables of those figures. The earlier benchmarks, which measured the same paths in other shapes, were removed; the `scale` soak tests stay.
